A five-hour nationwide stoppage of US flight departures forced thousands of cancellations as officials blamed data overload on aging analog air traffic control hardware.

WASHINGTON — The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) lifted a five-hour nationwide ground stop at 8:15 p.m. Eastern Standard Time on Friday after a telecommunications hardware failure disrupted air traffic control operations across the United States. The us flight ground stop stranded hundreds of thousands of travelers, forced more than 6,600 flight cancellations, and triggered nearly 10,000 flight delays worldwide as airlines scrambled to respond to the outage.

Key Facts

  • The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) grounded all domestic departures for approximately five hours due to telecommunications hardware failures.
  • Flight-tracking platform FlightAware reported at least 6,600 cancellations and 9,814 delays affecting flights within, into, and out of the United States.
  • More than 3,000 aircraft already airborne when the order was issued landed safely using secondary flight plans and backup radio systems.
  • The Department of Transportation requested $50 billion in emergency funding from Congress to replace aging analog infrastructure with modern internet protocol hardware.
  • Federal investigators found zero evidence of a cyberattack, hostile action, or software failures related to artificial intelligence systems.

Federal Aviation Administration Ground Stop Halts Departures Nationwide

Instability across nationwide air traffic control centers and Terminal Radar Approach Control (TRACON) facilities began at 3:36 p.m. Eastern Time, according to US Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy. Radar and communication systems began producing inconsistent data between ground controllers and aircraft in flight. In response, the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) instituted a nationwide order halting departing flights across all domestic airports to preserve safety.

Flight-tracking service FlightAware recorded 4,749 cancellations and 9,814 delays shortly after the ground stop, with later updates placing total cancellations above 6,600. Major hubs suffered severe disruptions: Chicago O'Hare International Airport recorded 395 cancellations, while Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport logged more than 350 canceled flights. Large international airports in Texas, New York, Boston, and Denver each reported over 200 cancellations.

Aviation analytics firm Cirium reported that at least 827 flights were diverted while the order was active. Among them, 670 aircraft returned to their departure airports, while 157 diverted to alternate airfields. Delta Air Lines Flight 958 from Los Angeles to New York turned around four hours into its flight to land in Cincinnati, while a Volaris flight from Mexico diverted to Tijuana just south of the US border.

US Flight Ground Stop Reason Points to Data Overload on Aging Systems

Addressing reporters at a White House news conference, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy attributed the failure to antiquated equipment. "What we have is an incredibly old air traffic control system," Duffy said, as reported by ABC Australia. "We have analogue telecoms, our routers actually got overloaded with data … and they started to give us inconsistencies in the data … because of that we shut the system down."

Duffy added that newer equipment installed under recent upgrades performed properly during the crisis. "I have already replaced some of those switches with President Trump, and our new IP switches did not go down," Duffy stated, according to Business Insider. "It's the old hardware that went down."

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin clarified that recent system updates may have contributed to the strain on legacy infrastructure. "As Secretary Duffy and FAA was going in and putting patches into the system, they were putting new equipment and new technology and that new equipment, new technology … was overwhelming the older system," Mullin said during the briefing, as reported by The Guardian. Mullin emphasized that "the public was never in danger" and noted that investigators found no signs of foul play. "We've seen zero evidence that this was a cyber-attack," Mullin told reporters.

Officials also rejected speculation linking the outage to a recent rollout of an artificial intelligence tool named Smart, developed by ASI to assist with aircraft rerouting. Both Duffy and ASI confirmed the software was not responsible for the equipment failure. Microsoft similarly issued a statement on X confirming its services were unrelated to the FAA ground stop following a separate 21-hour outage earlier in the week.

US Airline Ground Stop Triggers Global Delays and Diversions

The federal aviation administration grounding flights created immediate international ripples. The FAA requested that Eurocontrol suspend US-bound departures across its 42 member nations, impacting flights from countries including Israel, Morocco, and Türkiye. In Australia, Sydney Airport reported that all US-bound departures were temporarily held before normal operations resumed following the lifting of the FAA order.

Airlines operating in North America enacted major schedule reductions. Air Canada canceled all remaining flights to and from the US on Friday night, warning that international services passing through American airspace toward South America and the Caribbean faced delays. JetBlue canceled the remainder of its domestic schedule on Friday while attempting to preserve select transatlantic routes departing from New York and Boston.

"We have this significant problem of airplanes at higher altitudes that cannot communicate with air traffic control, so the solution to that is less airplanes up there. Keep everyone down, let everyone land."

— Shawn Pruchnik, Associate Professor at Ohio State University's Aviation Department, via ABC Australia

Retired Delta Air Lines pilot Mark Stephens told Business Insider that managing airborne traffic during an outage relies on standardized safety protocols. "Stay the course and do what you're trained: aviation, navigate, communicate," Stephens said, noting that aircraft continue along pre-filed flight plans using backup radio channels and onboard radar systems. However, Stephens warned of cascading logistical problems: "Long delays will turn into a crew rest nightmare. Tomorrow is going to be a very difficult reset as pilots and flight attendants will be all over the country, and planes will be stuck in cities without crews."

Ground transport alternatives also felt the strain. Rail operator Amtrak reported website and mobile app service disruptions on Friday evening as stranded air passengers sought train reservations, leading to longer call wait times.

White House Pushes for $50 Billion Infrastructure Overhaul

Following the system failure, the Trump administration expanded its federal budget request for air traffic control modernization. Having initially requested $30.1 billion earlier in the day, Secretary Duffy raised the emergency funding request to $50 billion during Friday evening's White House press briefing.

The previous $30.1 billion request outlined $10.1 billion for air traffic control technology—such as cloud computing, modern controller workstations, and common automation platforms—alongside $2 billion for control tower replacements and $18 billion for airport terminal upgrades. Congress had previously approved $12.5 billion for air traffic control upgrades in July 2025, alongside $1.9 billion specifically set aside to replace outdated en route centers.

Despite recent progress—including the FAA replacing 68% of its copper telecommunications lines with high-speed fiber infrastructure—the agency has long relied on legacy technology. FAA facilities have historically sourced replacement parts through online auction platforms and continue to rely on floppy disks at more than 400 control locations, with full phaseouts scheduled through 2028.

System Stability Verified as Airlines Scramble Crew and Aircraft

The FAA announced that extensive testing was completed before reopening US airspace at 8:15 p.m. Eastern Time. "Before clearing flights to resume, we thoroughly tested our systems to confirm they were stable and that operations remain reliable," the agency said in an official statement.

Transportation Secretary Duffy cautioned that the flight recovery process would extend well into the weekend. "Does that mean every flight is going to fly right now? It's going to be slow," Duffy told reporters. "We are going to try to move as many of those flights as we can tonight if we have capacity. If not, we are going to start tomorrow morning with our airline partners."

Federal agencies and congressional committees are set to begin formal reviews of the incident. The Senate Commerce Committee stated that Friday's events underscore the necessity of prioritizing en route traffic control centers, while House Homeland Security Committee lawmakers demanded that the Department of Transportation and DHS preserve all email communications and technical records for upcoming congressional oversight hearings.

Frequently Asked Questions

us flight ground stop status

The nationwide ground stop was officially lifted by the FAA at 8:15 p.m. Eastern Standard Time on Friday. While airspace is fully open, airlines warn that flight delays and cancellations will continue through the weekend as crews and planes are repositioned.

us flight ground stop reason

The ground stop was caused by a telecommunications failure in legacy air traffic control hardware. Aging analog switches and routers became overloaded with data, creating data inconsistencies that forced officials to halt departures as a safety precaution.

Was the FAA flight halt caused by a cyberattack?

No. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin confirmed there is zero evidence of a cyberattack, intelligence threat, or unauthorized access. Officials stated the issue was purely mechanical and technological.

How many flights were affected by the us airline ground stop?

FlightAware reported more than 6,600 flight cancellations and over 9,800 delays across the United States, while at least 827 flights in transit were forced to divert to alternate airports.
