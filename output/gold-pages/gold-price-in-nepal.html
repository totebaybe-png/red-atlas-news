Chhapawal (24K) vs Tejabi and 22K Gold Purity in Nepal Explained

Nepal's bullion and jewellery market uses distinctive traditional terminology alongside modern karat marks. The highest purity traded in the country is 24K fine gold (stamped 999 fineness), universally known in Nepali bazaars as Chhapawal gold. Historically named after the official assay stamp (chhap) pressed onto pure bullion bars, Chhapawal 24 carat gold is prized across Nepal not only for investment coins and biscuits, but also for crafting traditional high-purity Nepali bridal ornaments where families insist on maximum fineness.

Alongside Chhapawal sits Tejabi gold—derived from the process of acid refining—and 22K hallmarked jewellery gold (stamped 916 fineness). Because pure 24K gold is naturally soft and can bend during daily wear, jewellers blend pure metal with a small proportion of copper or silver alloy to create 22 carat gold, providing the structural strength needed for intricate chains, rings, and stone-set ornaments.

Whether you are checking the gold price in Nepal 24k for pure Chhapawal ornaments or the 22k gold price in Nepal today per tola for durable modern jewellery, understanding the distinction between 999 and 916 fineness is crucial. Always verify with your jeweller whether a piece is crafted from pure 24K Chhapawal or 22K alloyed metal, as both the initial rate and the future resale calculation depend directly on this grade.

  • 24K Chhapawal (999 fineness): Pure fine gold used in Nepal for bullion biscuits and traditional high-purity ornaments.
  • 22K / Tejabi (916 fineness): Durable working gold blended with alloy for intricate, long-lasting jewellery designs.
  • Nepali cultural preference uniquely favours high-karat (24K and 22K) ornaments for weddings and family heirlooms.
  • Confirm whether your invoice specifies 24K Chhapawal or 22K purity before finalizing any showroom transaction.

Traditional Nepali Gold Weights: Tola, Lal, Ana, and Metric Grams

Across Kathmandu Valley, the Terai plains, and hill districts alike, the undisputed benchmark weight for precious metals in Nepal is the tola. One standard tola equals 11.66 grams (specifically 11.6638 grams) on a calibrated electronic scale. Whenever Nepali news outlets, radio bulletins, or jewellery showrooms announce the daily sun chandi rate, the figure per 1 tola leads the report.

To weigh smaller items such as finger rings, earrings (top), or nose pins (phuli and bulaki), Nepali goldsmiths subdivide one tola into one hundred lals, and also reference the traditional ana (where sixteen anas equal one tola). Talking in lals is especially practical for everyday buyers because each lal represents exactly one-hundredth of a tola, making fractional pricing intuitive during showroom negotiations.

At the same time, official FENEGOSIDA tariff circulars and modern computerized billing systems always list rates per 10 grams and weigh items down to fractions of 1 gram. Because 10 grams is lighter than 1 tola (11.66 grams), buyers should check both the 10 grams and 1 tola rows in our price-by-weight table above to ensure accurate conversions between metric scales and traditional tola and lal quotes.

  • 1 Tola (11.66 grams): The universal household and market unit for quoting gold and silver in Nepal.
  • Lal: Traditional Nepali subdivision where one hundred lals equal exactly one tola.
  • Ana: Classic fractional measure equal to one-sixteenth of a tola, used for lightweight ornaments.
  • 10 Grams and 1 Gram: Official metric units displayed on digital scales and wholesale tariff sheets.

How FENEGOSIDA Sets Daily Rates and How the INR Peg Affects Nepali Gold

In Nepal, daily domestic retail bullion rates are coordinated by the Federation of Nepal Gold and Silver Dealers' Association (FENEGOSIDA). Each morning during the trading week, the association evaluates overnight international spot prices in US dollars, commercial bank import costs, customs duties, and regional market trends across neighbouring India to publish official per-tola and per-10-gram rates for Chhapawal and Tejabi gold.

A defining feature of the Nepali gold market is the fixed currency peg between the Nepalese rupee (NPR) and the Indian rupee (INR). Because the NPR is pegged at a constant exchange rate to the INR, any fluctuation of the Indian rupee against the US dollar immediately translates into an identical proportional movement in the NPR-to-USD exchange rate. Consequently, when readers search for the gold price today Nepal in Indian rupees or compare rates across the open border, both currencies move in tight synchronization.

Commercial banks authorised by Nepal Rastra Bank import bullion under daily quota limits and distribute bars to registered jewellery associations across the provinces. When festival or wedding demand outstrips the daily bank import quota, physical tightness can emerge in local bazaars, making live spot monitoring even more valuable for buyers.

  • FENEGOSIDA publishes official daily domestic rates for fine (Chhapawal) and working (Tejabi) gold in Nepal.
  • Because the Nepalese rupee is pegged to the Indian rupee, USD-to-INR currency moves directly impact NPR gold prices.
  • Nepal Rastra Bank regulates bullion imports through commercial bank quotas and customs tariffs.
  • Live spot tables track global market movements in real time between morning association announcements.

Making Charges (Jarti and Jyala) and Showroom Billing in Nepal

When purchasing traditional or modern ornaments in a Nepali jewellery shop, the final invoice exceeds the raw per-tola gold rate because of two traditional craftsmanship charges: jyala and jarti. Jyala represents the direct artisan labour fee paid to the goldsmith (karigar) for shaping, soldering, and polishing the piece, while jarti represents the wastage allowance calculated for tiny amounts of gold lost during melting, filing, and intricate handwork.

The amount of jyala and jarti depends heavily on the complexity of the ornament. Traditional Nepali cultural pieces—such as tilhari, naugedi, chandrahar, mangalsutra, sirbandi, and intricate Newari bridal ornaments—require hours of delicate manual artistry and therefore command higher jyala and jarti than simple machine-pressed rings or plain chains. Additionally, government tax budgets periodically adjust luxury levies or value-added charges on retail precious metal transactions.

When selling or exchanging old gold ornaments in Nepal, reputable jewellers buy back items bought from their own showroom with minimal deductions based on the day's live rate, though original jyala and jarti charges are not refunded. Always clarify the exact jarti percentage and buyback guarantee before placing a custom order.

  • Jyala is the artisan labour fee for crafting the ornament; jarti is the traditional melting and filing wastage allowance.
  • Handcrafted cultural ornaments like tilhari, chandrahar, and sirbandi carry higher jyala and jarti than plain bands.
  • Verify that glass beads (pote), threads, lac filling, and non-gold stones are excluded from net gold weight.
  • Confirm the showroom's written buyback policy—most shops offer top resale value on their own stamped receipts.

Seasonal Demand Drivers: Teej, Dashain, Tihar (Dhanteras), and Wedding Months

While global macroeconomic forces—such as US Federal Reserve interest rate decisions, global inflation, and central bank bullion buying—dictate the overarching trajectory of the gold price in Nepal, the domestic calendar creates pronounced seasonal demand waves. The first major surge arrives in late summer with Haritalika Teej, when married women and families across Nepal purchase gold tilhari, earrings, and bangles.

Autumn brings the back-to-back national festivals of Dashain and Tihar. During Tihar, the auspicious day of Dhanteras (Kag Tihar / Laxmi Puja preparation) sees massive crowds at jewellery showrooms in Kathmandu's New Road, Indrachowk, Patan, and Pokhara as households buy gold and silver coins, Lakshmi idols, and ornaments to invite prosperity into the home.

Furthermore, the auspicious Nepali wedding months—particularly Mangsir, Magh, Falgun, and Baishakh according to the Bikram Sambat calendar—generate peak demand for heavy bridal sets. Expatriate remittances sent home by Nepali workers in the Gulf, Malaysia, East Asia, and Western nations also provide steady household liquidity that supports year-round gold accumulation.

  • Haritalika Teej, Dashain, and Tihar (especially Dhanteras and Laxmi Puja) trigger peak festival gold buying.
  • Auspicious Bikram Sambat wedding months like Mangsir, Magh, Falgun, and Baishakh drive bridal showroom traffic.
  • Strong remittance inflows from Nepali workers abroad support household investment in physical gold.
  • Global US dollar spot trends and INR/NPR currency shifts determine the daily price direction.

Practical Tips for Buying Gold Ornaments and Bullion in Nepal

To ensure you receive genuine value when buying gold in Nepal, start by verifying purity and weight transparently. Whether you are buying 24K Chhapawal or 22K ornaments, ask the jeweller to demonstrate the purity using an electronic XRF testing machine or confirm the official hallmark stamp on the piece. When buying traditional pote necklaces like a tilhari, always make sure the gold cylinder is weighed separately before it is strung onto glass beads and cotton threads.

Second, pay close attention to unit conversions on the scale. Digital balances in Nepali showrooms display weight in metric grams, which the jeweller then converts into tola and lal on your bill. Use our live price-by-weight table and calculator above to double-check that the gram weight matches the stated tola and lal fraction accurately.

Finally, always insist on an official pan/tax bill (pakka bill) bearing the showroom's registered stamp, signature, exact net weight, purity classification (24K Chhapawal or 22K), itemised jyala and jarti charges, and explicit buyback terms. Keeping this original bill safe guarantees effortless liquidity if you ever need to exchange or sell the ornament in the future.

  • Weigh gold components like tilhari cylinders before stringing with pote beads, threads, or wax fillings.
  • Cross-check metric gram weights on the digital scale against tola and lal conversions on the receipt.
  • Negotiate jyala (labour) and jarti (wastage) transparently before commissioning custom wedding ornaments.
  • Always preserve the stamped showroom bill stating net weight, karat purity, and guaranteed buyback terms.
