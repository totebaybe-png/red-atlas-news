The 71-year-old pontiff rallied tens of thousands in South America's most secular nation following recent lung cancer surgery.

On Saturday, November 7, 2026, Pope Leo XIV celebrated his first open-air service in South America, drawing an estimated 40,000 worshippers to Parque París Londres in the western Uruguayan city of Paysandú. The pope leo uruguay mass marked the official start of a 12-day, three-nation tour across Latin America, coming just three weeks after the 71-year-old pontiff underwent surgery for lung cancer. Addressing pilgrims who gathered under blustery, rain-filled skies, the Roman Catholic leader urged attendees to become "bridge builders" and foster social unity across deep political and cultural divides.

Key Facts:

  • Event: Pope Leo XIV celebrated his inaugural South American Mass in Paysandú, Uruguay, launching a 12-day tour of the continent.
  • Attendance: According to Vatican officials, 30,000 worshippers pre-registered, while an additional 10,000 people arrived despite morning rain.
  • Health Status: The 71-year-old pontiff appeared in good health following his October 13 lung cancer surgery.
  • Cultural Imagery: The sermon incorporated regional traditions, utilizing mate tea and local river bridges as metaphors for faith and community.
  • Itinerary: The three-nation journey continues from Uruguay to Argentina and concludes in Peru.

Gathering at the Border: Worshippers Assemble in Paysandú

Located on the banks of the Uruguay River approximately 375 kilometers (230 miles) west of Montevideo, Paysandú was selected for the pontiff's first Mass due to its accessible location directly across from the Argentine city of Colón. According to reportage from ABC News and Al Jazeera, crowds greeted the pope with stadium chants of "Olé, olé, olé, olé, pope, pope!" as his aircraft arrived in the city. Faithful waved yellow handkerchiefs, sounded horns, and cheered as Pope Leo passed through Parque París Londres inside an open-sided popemobile.

Vatican authorities confirmed that more than 30,000 pilgrims from Uruguay, Argentina, and Brazil had registered in advance for the service, with another 10,000 turning out despite overcast skies and damp weather. For many regional travelers, crossing the international bridge into Paysandú provided a more accessible opportunity to view the pontiff than his upcoming itinerary stops.

Speaking to ABC News and Agence France-Presse, 73-year-old pilgrim Soledad Sánchez, who traveled from northern Uruguay, reflected on the pontiff's health challenges. "He’s the representative of Christ but he’s human. And we weren’t sure if he could make it, but he pushed through for us," Sánchez said. "He made it here to give us a message of peace because he knows our world needs it right now."

Pilgrims from neighboring nations echoed those sentiments. Luciana Lobato, a 20-year-old Argentine tour coordinator who arranged buses for 180 travelers across the border, told ABC News that "Argentina is a much more religious country than Uruguay, and there’s this desire to be here to witness something historic." Meanwhile, Laiana Córdoba, who drove 20 hours from Cascavel, Brazil, observed that the papal message resonated locally: "It’s something we understand, being neighbors means more than just sharing a border. It means finding ways to connect, opening paths to each other."

Mate Symbolism and Reaching Latin America's Most Secular Nation

In his homily, Pope Leo urged Uruguay's population of 3.5 million to turn to faith in confronting modern societal challenges, calling on attendees to "forward the mission of evangelisation with enthusiasm" and build a "fraternal community that comes together and shares life, projects, dreams and hopes," as reported by Al Jazeera. He reminded the crowd that Catholic traditions had been "part of the life of the Uruguayan people since their earliest days, and it must be passed on to future generations with creative courage!"

The pontiff drew warm applause by honoring mate, the traditional hot herbal tea widely consumed across Uruguay, Argentina, and Paraguay. Uruguayans consume roughly 9 kilograms (about 20 pounds) of dried mate leaf per person annually—the highest per capita rate in the world, according to industry estimates cited by ABC News. The bitter infusion is traditionally steeped in a hollowed gourd and passed hand-to-hand among friends and strangers through a metal straw known as a bombilla.

Paying homage to the communal custom, Pope Leo noted that mate "teaches us to share and be together," adding humorously that "Mate has even made its way to Rome," France 24 reported. "We must reflect on these symbols that we share, which give us identity and a sense of belonging, which speak to us of our origins and point us toward the future," the pope declared.

The pontiff's cultural outreach found reception among secular residents in Uruguay, where a Latinobarometro poll showed the regional Catholic population has dropped from 80 percent to 55 percent over the past three decades. Paysandú resident Jorge Monzón, 64, told ABC News: "I’m not religious. I’d never go to Mass if it weren’t the pope, but he surprised me. He made it appealing, the way he talked about things we all have in common, like mate. I personally felt a sense of communion." Similarly, 18-year-old Colombian resident Santiago Biglieri remarked to DW, "I wouldn’t call myself religious, but I like this pope. He has a really good message for humanity. Even for the nonbelievers, there’s something to take from his ideas."

Healthcare Advocacy, Medical Recovery, and End-of-Life Dignity

Saturday's service was dedicated to Blessed Jacinto Vera, who was appointed Uruguay's first bishop by Pope Leo XIII in 1878. During remarks delivered on Friday in Montevideo, Pope Leo praised Bishop Vera's rural ministry to the impoverished, noting that those he met "found something of Christ's goodness" in him, according to DW reporting.

The tour represents the 71-year-old American pontiff's first public travel since undergoing lung cancer surgery on October 13. En route from Rome to Montevideo, Pope Leo informed journalists that his rapid recovery was attributable to "trusting God, good doctors, good surgeons, and overall, I think a healthy lifestyle," Al Jazeera reported.

His recent personal health experiences framed his public statements on social policy. In a speech delivered Friday in Montevideo, Pope Leo advocated for universal healthcare access. France 24 reported that the pontiff cited his own cancer diagnosis when reflecting on "many men and women" who suffer without receiving timely medical diagnoses or care.

Without directly naming Uruguay's legislation legalizing assisted suicide last year, Pope Leo addressed end-of-life care, asserting that society must support individuals to "live with dignity, receive the care they need, and be accompanied until the natural end of his or her life." He stated that the Catholic Church seeks to "alleviate suffering whenever possible" while reinforcing to the ill that "their lives always retain meaning."

Navigating Regional Political Shifts and Ideological Fractures

Pope Leo's journey unfolds against a backdrop of shifting political alignments across Latin America. Several right-wing administrations have assumed power across the continent since United States President Donald Trump returned to the White House in January of last year. In contrast, left-wing Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva secured a fourth presidential term last month, according to Al Jazeera.

The pontiff's diplomatic schedule includes meetings with leaders holding starkly different ideological views. Earlier this year, President Trump publicly criticized Pope Leo after the pontiff issued statements condemning the US-Israeli war on Iran, France 24 reported. On Sunday, Pope Leo will travel to Argentina to meet President Javier Milei, a self-described anarcho-capitalist and free-market proponent aligned with Trump. Milei previously directed sharp public criticism at the pontiff regarding his positions on global conflicts and immigration policies.

What Happens Next on the Latin American Tour

Following Saturday morning's Mass, Pope Leo returned to Montevideo to inspect church-backed community development programs. The pontiff scheduled a visit to Casavalle, a capital neighborhood experiencing severe gang violence and high homicide rates, where the Church collaborates with municipal authorities to build housing and operate addiction rehabilitation facilities.

On Saturday evening, Pope Leo was scheduled to lead a night-time prayer vigil for an estimated 60,000 young attendees at Montevideo's Centenary Stadium, calling on youth to reject religious indifference. The event marks the conclusion of the first papal visit to Uruguay in nearly 40 years.

On Sunday, the pontiff departs for Argentina, where his itinerary includes official stops in Buenos Aires, Luján, and Córdoba. Pope Leo will conclude his 12-day continent tour in Peru, where he previously served as a bishop, holds citizenship, and spent nearly two decades operating as a missionary.
